# Script and retention (write before any voice is generated)

Adapted from the CURIOPEDIA production skill (methods only, no characters or brand). Use it for every narrated
Short or long form before generating the voice-over. Every pattern here is an editorial hypothesis until the
channel's own retention data confirms it.

## 1. Build the promise first
Before the draft, write down three things:
- the **object** the viewer will follow,
- the **question** the first shot opens,
- the **discovery** the episode ends on.

The promise must be understandable from the first framing with the sound off. Resolve it inside the episode.

Write three one-line openings, each with one picture. Pick the one with a concrete action, an anomaly or a
consequence that raises a question (someone does something that looks absurd, an object behaves unexpectedly, a
solution causes a new problem). Hand over the chosen one unless the user asks for options. Keep any channel intro
out of the way of the story's momentum. Soften absolute claims to what the evidence supports.

## 2. Pick a structure

| Structure | How it develops |
| --- | --- |
| Strange decision that makes sense | Show a choice, plant a clue, raise its consequences, reveal why it works |
| Mystery with a hidden mechanism | Show a result that is hard to explain, follow clues, reveal how it happened |
| Test that changes an intuition | Set an expectation, show how it is tested, explain what the result changes |
| Hypothesis with a personal cost | State a rule, follow its consequences, end on a decision born from the problem |
| Escalating solutions | Each fix changes the problem and demands a new answer, up to a final consequence |
| Social rule with an unexpected effect | Make a rule visible through one person, reveal the incentive driving it |

These fit the inkbeat-pop formats: *story* (any), *curiosity* (mystery, test), *misunderstood genius* (strange
decision, escalating solutions). Change topic, characters, wording and staging so the story is your own.

## 3. Progress through causes
- For each section, note what changed and which question stays open. Link the next section with a consequence, an
  obstacle, a test or a new reading, shown through action.
- Plant **one clue** early that the viewer recognizes at the end.
- Use two or three shifts in difficulty or understanding when the length allows; every shift must add information.
  In a ~10 s Short, concentrate on **one transformation that completes**.
- Vary the devices: an expectation fails, a solution has a cost, a small observation becomes important.

## 4. Reveal with visible proof
The explanation answers what the scene just raised. Use an everyday analogy only when it clarifies the mechanism,
and say which part of the comparison stands for the phenomenon. Then show the mechanism or its consequence on the
real object. End on a consequence, image or line that returns to the opening promise (Shorts: cut ~0.7 s after the
last word, no sign-off, so it loops).

## 5. Write for the voice and the picture
- Speakable sentences, concrete verbs, one idea per sentence. Put the decisive word where the voice and a gesture
  can stress it (it is a natural candidate for the yellow accent caption word).
- Plan two coordinated tracks: what the voice says and what the picture adds (anticipate a clue, show a contact,
  add a reaction). The object or action is on screen when it is named.
- **Every physical verb becomes a full action on screen** (see shot-review.md, "Sentence to action").
- For a second language, adapt the hook and payoff for the ear rather than translating; time each language to its
  own voice-over.

## 6. Facts and dramatization
Check every central claim against reliable, preferably primary sources; record link, figure, unit and date.
Check numbers, absolutes, laws, experiments and science especially. Separate fact, hypothesis and metaphor in the
wording. Label recreations as recreations. Do not attribute dialogue, intentions or crimes to real people without
sourcing; use fictional characters from the film's character family to show a mechanism. Prefer evergreen topics
for a catalog channel; keep current metrics used for research out of the narration.

## 7. Editorial review before voice

| Criterion | Evidence in the script |
| --- | --- |
| First image and hook | An object or action is visible and it is clear what is curious about it |
| Promise | There is one concrete question the episode answers |
| Causality | Each section follows from the previous one and changes the situation or its understanding |
| Progression | Changes bring consequences, tests or a new difficulty |
| Clue | A planted detail gains meaning at the payoff |
| Reveal | The explanation answers what was just observed |
| Ending | The last image or line completes the opening promise |
| Oral and visual clarity | The narration can be spoken and every action can be staged |
| Evidence | Central claims are sourced; hypotheses are framed as such |
| Originality | Story and wording are your own angle |

Find the two weakest sections and rewrite them. Keep next to the script: opening question, clue, reveal, ending
and sources. Re-check the first shot and the payoff again in the final cut.

## 8. Measure what works
Compare episodes of similar topic, length and age. Look at viewed-vs-swiped, average view duration and drops in
the retention curve; match each drop's second to the sentence and the picture on screen at that moment. Test one
concrete change in the next episodes and log the result.

Deliver with the script: title, clean voice-over text, sources and a shot table (shot-review.md).
